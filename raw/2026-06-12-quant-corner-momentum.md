[00:10] **Dana:** Welcome back to Quant Corner. Today: momentum — the factor that refuses to
die. The simple version is that assets that have gone up over the past 6 to 12 months tend
to keep outperforming over the next few months. It's one of the most robust empirical
patterns in finance, documented across stocks, bonds, currencies, and commodities.

[03:40] **Dana:** The canonical academic reference is Asness, Moskowitz and Pedersen's 2013
paper "Value and Momentum Everywhere" in the Journal of Finance. Cliff Asness co-founded AQR
Capital, and momentum has been a core part of their factor research for decades. The headline
finding is that value and momentum are negatively correlated, so combining them smooths the
ride — when one is struggling, the other often isn't.

[11:25] **Dana:** Now, the catch. Momentum has fat left tails. Daniel and Moskowitz called
them "momentum crashes." The worst episodes come in sharp market rebounds after a crash —
spring 2009 is the textbook case. The strategy is short the prior losers, and in a violent
recovery those losers rip higher, so the short side blows up. So momentum is not free; you're
being paid for taking crash risk.

[18:05] **Dana:** How do you actually own it? The simplest retail vehicle is a momentum ETF.
iShares MTUM — the MSCI USA Momentum Factor ETF — is the best-known one in the US. It's
cheap, with an expense ratio somewhere in the low-teens of basis points. It reconstitutes
semi-annually but can also rebalance after big volatility spikes. The tradeoff with any
ETF wrapper is that the rebalancing is rules-based and infrequent, so it's a watered-down,
lower-turnover version of academic momentum.

[24:30] **Dana:** My take: momentum is a real, persistent premium, but it's a diversifier,
not a standalone. Pair it with value, size it for the crash risk, and don't expect it to
work every year. Over the last decade it's had long flat stretches. That's the price of
admission.
