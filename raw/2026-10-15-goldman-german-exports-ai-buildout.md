Germany’s exports increased in the first half of 2026 and are providing a modest boost to GDP growth in Europe’s largest economy, according to Goldman Sachs Research . Our economists forecast that real exports will grow at a “robust” rate of 3.5% in 2026 and 2.4% the following year. Recent export strength reflects resilient foreign demand alongside modest, productivity-driven improvements in competitiveness. Goldman Sachs Research expects German exports to increase, driven by revived trade with the US and the European Union, plus the global artificial intelligence (AI) buildout. “Taken together, we are more optimistic about German export performance than at the start of 2026,” writes Niklas Garnadt, an economist in Goldman Sachs Research, in a report. Strong export performance in the first half of 2026 is consistent with healthy foreign demand and a modest improvement in relative unit labor costs, driven by stronger productivity growth. Garnadt cautions that competition from Chinese exporters is expected to continue weighing on German exports in the medium term, especially in third markets. Are German exports getting more competitive? Still, the brighter outlook marks a shift for a sector that has been a drag on growth since the onset of the European energy crisis in 2022, writes Garnadt. Increases in relative unit labor costs and the real effective exchange rate have recently taken a toll on German competitiveness. The near-term outlook for exports has “improved materially,” Garnadt writes. Survey-based, forward-looking export indicators have risen to their highest levels since the start of the European energy crisis. Hard data show that foreign manufacturing sales have risen since the start of the year, alongside a recovery in real goods exports. Foreign orders have increased even more sharply. This suggests further export growth in the quarters to come, according to Goldman Sachs Research. What’s driving Germany’s export turnaround? In addition to improved competitiveness, German exports to the US are recovering from the decline triggered last year by US tariffs. While exports in the second quarter remained materially below pre-tariff levels, the shortfall peaked in the second half of 2025 and has narrowed since then, writes Garnadt. Recent monthly economic data point to further improvement in the third quarter. Trade with the EU is also rebounding after the slump that followed the energy crunch four years ago. “Since early 2025, EU trade has become a significant tailwind, reflected in rising manufacturing sales and orders,” writes Garnadt. Demand for AI infrastructure boosts key German sectors Thanks to the development of AI infrastructure worldwide, German computer, electronics, and electrical equipment companies are making meaningful contributions to export growth.
