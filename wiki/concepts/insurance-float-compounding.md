# Insurance-float compounding (the Berkshire model)

## Definition
Using an **insurance company's float** — premiums collected now against claims paid later — as
**low-cost (sometimes negative-cost) investable capital**, then compounding it by investing the
proceeds well. The engine behind Berkshire Hathaway, and the model [[bill-ackman]] is rebuilding
inside [[hhh|Howard Hughes]].

## How it works
- Write policies → collect premiums up front → hold the **float** until claims come due. If
  underwriting at least breaks even, that float is **free leverage**.
- **Two balance-sheet jobs:** the *liability* side (underwrite risk) and the *asset* side
  (invest the money). Most insurers obsess over liabilities; Buffett's edge was treating it as an
  **investing** vehicle.
- Buffett's split (per the interview): **policyholder float → short-term treasuries** (no risk),
  **surplus/equity → common stocks**. Compounds tax-efficiently for decades.

## Pros / cons
- **Pro:** permanent, cheap capital; tax-efficient compounding; durable if underwriting is
  disciplined and the investor is genuinely good.
- **Con:** needs **real investing talent inside an insurer** (rare — the talent goes to hedge
  funds, which is *why* few replicate Berkshire). Underwriting losses turn float costly;
  long-duration and rate-sensitive.

## Who uses it
- **Warren Buffett / Berkshire Hathaway** — the template (textile co → insurance → invest float).
- **[[bill-ackman]] / [[hhh]]** — converting a real-estate compounder into an insurer-led holding
  company (Vantage acquisition) to do the same over a 50-year horizon.
